How the score is derived

01Two questions, two engines

The triage tool answers two questions that people routinely conflate but that are, statistically and practically, independent. Priority asks how much governance attention a system deserves relative to everything else the organisation runs. Risk asks how likely harm is, and how severe, given the controls actually in place today.

The first is largely a property of what the system is for — who it touches and how much is at stake. The second is a property of how it is built and run, and it moves the moment a control is strengthened or weakened. Because the two vary along different axes, each has its own engine, its own aggregation logic, and its own output scale. The rest of this document explains both.

02The Priority engine — the AGPI

The Adaptive Governance Priority Index (AGPI) is a weighted linear value model, the same family of technique used in multi-attribute value theory (Keeney & Raiffa, 1976; Belton & Stewart, 2002). Six dimensions are each scored on an anchored 1–5 scale, rescaled to a common 0–1 range, weighted by importance, and summed to a single index on 0–100.

Step 1 — normalise each dimension

Raw scores run 1 to 5. Each is mapped to the unit interval by min–max normalisation, so that a score of 1 contributes nothing and a score of 5 contributes the dimension's full weight:

normalised = (score − 1) / 4  → range 0 to 1

This is what lets six differently-anchored scales be added together meaningfully: after normalisation they are all on the same footing, and the weights alone decide how much each one counts.

Step 2 — weight and sum

AGPI = Σ ( (score − 1) / 4 ) × weight  → 0 to 100

The weights are fixed by design and sum to exactly 100, so the index reads directly as a 0–100 figure with no further rescaling:

Public trust20Outward-facing

The 65 % design choice

The three outward-facing dimensions carry 65 of the 100 points. That is deliberate. It means the external consequences of a system — its effect on residents, on public trust, and on legal exposure — dominate its priority, while the internal dimensions can only modulate the result, never override it. A system cannot be pushed to the top of the queue on visibility and strategic ambition alone.

Step 3 — band the index

The continuous index is cut into five priority bands. Where a score falls on a boundary or carries a fractional part, it is resolved to the higher band — a precautionary rule that always errs toward more scrutiny, not less.

AGPIBand
80–100Priority 1 — Critical
60–79Priority 2 — High
40–59Priority 3 — Standard
20–39Priority 4 — Routine
below 20Priority 5 — Observe

Behaviour worth knowing

Because the model is linear, its sensitivity is fixed and transparent: a one-point change on any dimension moves the index by exactly a quarter of that dimension's weight. Moving resident impact by one point shifts the score by 6.25; moving oversight by one point shifts it by only 2.5. Nothing is hidden in interactions or thresholds. The index is monotonic (raising any input never lowers the score), bounded (always 0–100), and compensatory (a low score on one dimension can be offset by a high score on another). That last property is intentional here, and it is precisely the property the Risk engine is built not to have — see section 04.

03The Risk engine

The Risk engine follows the likelihood-by-impact tradition familiar from the standard risk matrix and from risk-management standards such as ISO 31000:2018, but with two deliberate refinements: it takes the worst impact rather than the average, and it discounts for control effectiveness to produce a residual figure rather than a raw one.

Step 1 — inherent risk

Five impact categories are each scored 1–5. The engine takes the single highest of the five as the impact figure and multiplies it by likelihood:

inherent = likelihood × max(five impact categories)  → 1 to 25

Taking the maximum, not the mean, is the key statistical decision. A system that is catastrophic on safety but benign everywhere else must not have that catastrophe averaged away by its four low scores. Impact here is non-compensatory: the worst plausible harm sets the level.

Step 2 — residual risk

Inherent risk is then scaled by how well the system is actually controlled. Control effectiveness is scored on an inverse 1–5 scale — 1 is very strong control, 5 is ineffective — so the multiplier runs from 0.2 to 1.0:

residual = inherent × (control effectiveness / 5)  → 0.2 to 25

Strong control (1) cuts inherent risk to a fifth; ineffective control (5) leaves it untouched. This is the term that makes risk control-dependent: change nothing about the system's purpose, tighten only its controls, and the residual figure falls immediately.

Step 3 — tier the residual

As with priority, boundaries resolve to the higher tier.

ResidualTier
1–5Low
6–10Medium
11–15High
16–25Critical

Being multiplicative, the engine's sensitivity is contextual rather than fixed: a one-point change in likelihood matters more when impact is high, because the two terms interact. That is a faithful reflection of how risk actually behaves — raising the odds of a minor event is not the same as raising the odds of a severe one.

04Sum vs maximum — why the two engines aggregate differently

This is the most important design point, and the one most worth being able to explain. The two engines use opposite aggregation philosophies, on purpose.

  • Priority is compensatory. It is a weighted sum. A modest score on one dimension can be balanced by a strong score on another, because priority is a judgement about overall salience, where trade-offs across dimensions are meaningful and intended.
  • Risk is non-compensatory on impact. It takes the maximum. No amount of low scores elsewhere can dilute a single severe harm, because when it comes to potential damage the tail is what matters, not the average.

Using a sum for priority and a maximum for impact is not an inconsistency in the framework; it is the framework encoding two genuinely different kinds of judgement in the mathematics itself.

05Escalation triggers — a non-compensatory hard gate

Two independent axes create a risk of their own: a genuinely dangerous system could sit quietly low on both scores. Seven escalation triggers guard against exactly that. They operate as a logical OR — a disjunctive override — sitting on top of the risk engine:

if any trigger is met → risk tier = at least High
    if statutory or unreviewed agentic → at least Critical
    regardless of the calculated scores

Special-category data, statutory decisions, and agentic action without per-action human review are three of the seven. Any single one floors the risk tier (to Critical for statutory or unreviewed agentic action, otherwise High) and mandates Board approval and comprehensive assurance. Statistically this is a knock-out criterion: a hard constraint that cannot be traded off or averaged away, deliberately placed outside the scoring so that specific, well-understood hazards can never be scored into invisibility. The higher-band rounding rule performs a gentler version of the same protective job at every boundary.

06Why not collapse it to one number

It is tempting to multiply or average priority and risk into a single headline figure. Doing so would destroy information the governance team needs.

The two measures are close to orthogonal: knowing a system's priority tells you little about its residual risk, and vice versa. A high-priority system with excellent controls can be low risk; a low-priority back-office system with a real safety exposure and weak controls can be high risk. Collapsing two orthogonal coordinates into one is a lossy projection — it is like averaging a map's latitude and longitude and expecting to still find the place. The classic failure mode it prevents is the quietly dangerous system: low salience, high residual risk, invisible under any blended score. Keeping the axes apart is what makes that system visible.

The short version

Priority is roughly control-independent — it is a function of purpose variables, none of which are controls. Risk is control-dependent — control effectiveness is one of its inputs. That single structural fact is why the two come apart, and why they are reported as two coordinates rather than one.

07Limits and honest caveats

The method is structured decision support, not a decision, and it rests on assumptions worth stating plainly.

  • Ordinal inputs treated as interval. The 1–5 scales are ordinal judgements. The arithmetic treats the gaps between them as equal, which is a modelling convenience, not a measured fact (Hubbard & Evans, 2010). Well-anchored scale descriptions reduce the distortion but do not remove it.
  • Risk matrices have known weaknesses. Multiplying ordinal likelihood and impact can, in general, rank some risks inconsistently and compress a wide range of real risks into the same cell (Cox, 2008; Thomas et al., 2014). The framework mitigates this by taking the worst impact rather than the average, by keeping risk separate from priority, and above all by the escalation gate — but the underlying caution about risk matrices still applies, and the tier should never be read as a precise quantity.
  • Weights are judgements. The weight vector encodes a policy choice about what matters. It is defensible and fixed for consistency, but it is not empirically derived, and reasonable organisations could set it differently.
  • Garbage in, garbage out. Every output is only as good as the scores entered. The tool makes the reasoning explicit and repeatable; it does not make the underlying assessment correct.

Used as intended — to triage consistently, to make the reasoning visible, and to route systems to the right level of scrutiny — these limits are manageable. Used as an oracle, they are not. The point of the method is to structure human judgement, not to replace it.
